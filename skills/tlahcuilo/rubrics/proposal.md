# Rubric — Pitch / proposal

For persuasive copy that asks a reader to **back something**: funding pitches, program proposals,
product narratives put in front of a ministry, funder, partner, or decision-maker. The reader's
test is not "is this well-written" but "do I believe this, and do I know what I'm being asked to
back." Often a mixed audience — a decision-maker who controls budget plus the technical people who
will vet the claim. Every panelist scores against these criteria and argues from them; a position
that doesn't map to a criterion is out of scope.

## Criteria

1. **clear-value** — Is the core value proposition unmistakable on one read? The reader can say,
   in a sentence, what this does and why it's better than what exists. Flag copy where the "so
   what" is buried, implied, or arrives only at the end.
2. **credible-claims** — Every claim the copy makes is one the product can actually support. No
   claim inflated past what the technology or evidence delivers; distinctive claims ("we do X, they
   don't") are true and checkable. Flag overreach, vague superlatives, and anything a skeptical
   technical reviewer would puncture.
3. **stakes-clear** — Is it clear why this matters and to whom — the problem being solved and who
   is hurt by the status quo? Flag copy that describes a mechanism without ever landing why the
   reader should care.
4. **objections-handled** — Does the copy anticipate the obvious pushback (cost, feasibility, "is
   this just X rebranded", risk to the end user) and defuse it, or does it leave the reader's first
   objection unanswered? Flag the unaddressed elephant.
5. **concrete-not-abstract** — Are claims grounded in specific, tangible detail (a real behavior, a
   named contrast, a scenario) rather than abstraction and adjectives? Flag "powerful",
   "innovative", "deeper" doing work that a concrete example should do.
6. **emotional-truth** — Does the copy connect to the real human stakes without manipulation or
   sentimentality? For mission-driven pitches, the emotional line must be earned and honest, not
   pandering. Flag both the cold spec-sheet and the saccharine overreach.
7. **one-read-decidable** — After one pass, does the reader know what they're being asked to do,
   believe, or fund — and could they repeat the pitch to someone else? Flag copy that informs but
   doesn't move, or that ends without a point.

## Scoring (joust mode)

Score each criterion 1–5: **5** exemplary · **4** solid, minor gaps · **3** usable, real gaps ·
**2** would mislead or lose the reader · **1** absent/broken.

## What debate should surface here

- A value proposition that only becomes clear in the last paragraph — or never.
- A claim stated more strongly than the product can back (the technical reviewer's veto).
- Mechanism described in loving detail while the stakes go unstated.
- The obvious objection (cost, "isn't this just a fancy WPM counter", privacy of children's data)
  left hanging.
- Abstraction and promotional adjectives standing in for a concrete example.
- Emotional appeal that tips into manipulation, or a pitch so dry it doesn't move anyone.
- Copy that reads well but doesn't actually ask for anything or leave a decision in the reader's hands.
